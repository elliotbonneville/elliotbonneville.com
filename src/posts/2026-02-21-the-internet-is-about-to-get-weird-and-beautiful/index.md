---
title: The Internet Is About to Get Weird and Beautiful
date: 2026-02-21
description: AI commoditized the boring startup landing page. What comes next is weirder, more personal, and more beautiful than anything we've had before.
---

AI made it trivial to build a website. Which means the gradient-to-white hero section with a sans-serif tagline and a "Get Started" button is now the default output of zero effort. The new blank page. The visual equivalent of saying nothing.

AI makes self-expression just as cheap as it made conformity, though. Image generation, code, layout, typography, ambient sound. AI lowered the bar, but it also raised the ceiling. The barrier to putting *yourself* onto the internet just collapsed. The only differentiator left is you.

A local bookshop whose website feels like stepping through the front door. Warm oak shelves lining the margins, a bell that chimes softly when you arrive, hand-lettered staff picks that shuffle every visit. There's a reading nook in the corner where the owner blogs in longhand script. Before AI, that's a five-figure agency project. Now it's an afternoon.

A ceramicist whose portfolio page *is* the kiln. Earth-toned, textured like wet clay. Each piece emerges as you scroll, rotating slowly so you can see the glaze catch the light. The background shifts with the time of day where she lives. Warm amber at golden hour. Cool blue at midnight.

A neighborhood restaurant that ditches the PDF menu for something that feels like sitting down at the table. Linen textures. Candlelight pooling at the edges. The daily specials in the chef's actual handwriting, regenerated each morning by an AI trained on her script.

A teenager builds a fan page for a book series that looks like the book itself. Parchment backgrounds, illuminated drop caps, marginalia that other fans scribble in, an interactive map you can lose an hour in. She told an AI what she saw in her head and it built it with her.

A musician whose homepage isn't a Linktree. It's a dim room. His recordings drift out of an old speaker rendered in the corner. The walls are covered in torn flyers from past shows. You navigate by walking through it. Built in a weekend.

A grandparent's memorial site that feels like opening a cedar chest in the attic. Sepia photographs that unfold gently, handwritten letters you can page through, a recording of her voice playing if you find the locket tucked into the corner of the page. Built by a granddaughter who can't code. She just described what she wanted it to feel like.

None of these were possible until recently. You needed a designer, a developer, a photographer, an illustrator. Tens of thousands of dollars and weeks of back-and-forth to get something that captured what you actually meant. Now you need taste and a conversation.

We spent fifteen years flattening the web into the same sans-serif rectangle because that was the only shape most people could afford. We called it minimalism. Really it was just a budget constraint in a turtleneck.

That constraint just lifted. The people on the other side of it are bookshop owners and ceramicists and teenagers and grandchildren with something specific to say and a way to finally say it.

The internet is about to get weird, and wonderful, and personal, and beautiful.
