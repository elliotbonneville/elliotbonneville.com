---
title: Now Make It Weirder
date: 2026-02-18
description: AI defaults to the most statistically average sentence. "Make it weirder" is the brute-force fix.
---

I have a confession to make.

Earlier today I wrote a post called "The Only Moat Left Is Money." It went to #1 on Hacker News. 190 points, 255 comments. One person out of 255 wrote: "To me this article reads not fully human."

They weren't wrong.

The post was AI-assisted. The ideas were mine. I had the core thesis in the bathroom, thinking about a tweet I'd seen and the 14 people who'd signed up for a thing I launched. Claude wrote the prose. I didn't even type the prompts. The whole thing was voice-dictated. I talked, it wrote, and then I sat there for maybe ten minutes saying "make it weirder" over and over until it stopped reading like a press release about my own thoughts.

That phrase did more work than anything else in the process. More than the prompt engineering, more than the system instructions, more than the examples of my previous writing I fed it for style matching.

AI writes the most statistically average version of every sentence. That's what it's doing, literally. A probability engine that picks the most likely next word given everything before it. The result is prose that's never wrong and never alive. Every sentence is the expected sentence. The metaphors are the first ones you'd think of.

Stylometry researchers have a word for what's missing: burstiness. Human writing has high variance in sentence length and complexity. Short punch. Then a longer one that takes its time, lets you lean in. Then short again. AI text is uniform. Same length, same rhythm, same register, all the way down. You can measure this with tools and you can feel it without them.

"Make it weirder" is a brute-force fix for this.

When you tell an AI to make something weirder, you're pushing it off the center of its probability distribution. "Compelling" becomes "had teeth." "She expressed her frustration" becomes "she slammed the door so hard the deadbolt clicked." Researchers call this "earned surprise." A conciliatory breeze. Arrogant flowers. Pairings the model would never produce on its own because they're statistically unlikely. That's exactly why they work.

The problem is it takes about 250 rounds.

You write a draft. It sounds like a LinkedIn post written by a thoughtful friend of a friend. You say make it weirder. Now it sounds like a LinkedIn post with one unusual adjective. You say make it weirder again. And again. Paragraph by paragraph, you drag each sentence away from the median until something starts to breathe. It's tedious and manual. It works.

I'm being honest about this because I think the conversation around AI-assisted writing is stuck in a useless place. One side says AI writing is garbage, you can always tell, anyone who uses it is lazy. The other side says AI writes better than most humans, get over it. Neither is right and both are missing the interesting part.

This argument isn't new. We had it about the typewriter.

Heidegger, 1942:

> The typewriter tears writing from the essential realm of the hand, i.e., the realm of the word. Mechanical writing deprives the hand of its rank in the realm of the written word. The typewriter makes everyone look the same.

Not just inconvenient — existentially violent. The machine didn't merely change how writing looked. It severed the connection between the writer's body and the writer's words. Kittler picked up the thread fifty years later:

> In standardized texts, paper and body, writing and soul fall apart. Typewriters do not store individuals.

C.S. Lewis told a student in 1959:

> Don't use a typewriter. The noise will destroy your sense of rhythm, which still needs years of training.

Graham Greene:

> My two fingers on a typewriter have never connected with my brain. My hand on a pen does. A fountain pen, of course.

Capote, in the Paris Review:

> No, I don't use a typewriter. Not in the beginning. I write my first version in longhand.

The diagnosis sounds familiar. A technology that takes something human and makes it average. Standardized. Expected.

But that's only half the story. Mark Twain got his hands on a Remington in 1874 and immediately wrote his brother — on the machine, all lowercase, typos and all:

> the machine has several virtues i believe it will print faster than i can write... it dont muss things or scatter ink blots around. of course it saves paper.

Henry James went further. He didn't just use a typewriter — he couldn't work without one. In 1897 he described the dependency:

> The click of the typewriter to which I dictate, and which, some months ago crept into my existence through the crevice of a lame hand, now occupies in it a place too big to be left vacant.

His typist, Theodora Bosanquet, later wrote that "the click of a Remington machine acted as a positive spur" to his composition. Nietzsche, going blind and writing on a Malling-Hansen Writing Ball, put it most concisely in an 1882 letter:

> Unser Schreibzeug arbeitet mit an unseren Gedanken.
>
> Our writing tools work along with our thoughts.

The minimalist poet Aram Saroyan made the machine itself a collaborator:

> I write on a typewriter, almost never in hand (I can hardly handwrite, I tend to draw words), and my machine — an obsolete red-top Royal Portable — is the biggest influence on my work.

Martyn Lyons spent a book documenting this pattern across hundreds of writers. Some were unsettled by the new distance between thought and mechanical output. But others developed what Lyons calls a "romantic" relationship to the machine:

> Writers trained in typewriter skills appear to have been more likely to develop a "romantic" relationship to the typewriter, seeing it as an extension of their bodies and even a source of inspiration.

Kerouac typed *On the Road* on a continuous scroll so he wouldn't have to stop to change pages. A Finnish poet named Aaro Hellaakoski used the typewriter's physical constraints — its grid, its monospace, its inability to draw — as a generative device for concrete poetry. Pulkkinen documented his process:

> A multiphase process of trial and error, where Hellaakoski strives to achieve a satisfactory visual rendering for his poem.

The limitation became the aesthetic. Wershler-Henry documented an entire subculture of literary experimentation — Burroughs' cut-ups, Kerouac's scroll, concrete poetry, typewriter art — that existed because the machine forced writers to work differently.

> Typewriting died a violent death, and violent deaths lead to hauntings.

The pattern is always the same. A new writing tool arrives. Philosophers diagnose it as the death of authentic expression. Writers ignore the philosophers and figure out what the tool can do that nothing else could. The pen was going to destroy memory (Plato said so). The printing press was going to destroy authority. The typewriter was going to destroy the hand. Each time, the anxiety was real and the prediction was wrong. Not because the tool was harmless — it did change things — but because the change opened as much as it closed.

The interesting part is the seam.

The thinking was mine. The thesis about attention and money came from a real fear I've been sitting with for weeks. The Josh Pigford tweet connection happened because I saw it at 10am and felt a specific pang of recognition. The line about 14 people signing up came from an actual experience that week, a number small enough to feel fragile. None of that was generated. The AI can't have those experiences. It can't sit with a fear. It can't feel a pang.

But it can write a clean sentence about them faster than I can. And when I say "make it weirder," it pushes that clean sentence somewhere I wouldn't have gone on my own. Not always somewhere better. But somewhere different. And different is the starting material for good.

The honest version of AI-assisted writing is this: the thinking is the hard part, the prose is the easy part, and "make it weirder" is how you close the gap between what the machine produces and what a human would actually write. It's not a shortcut. I spent longer on the moat post than I would have writing it myself. The time just moved. Instead of staring at a blank page, I was staring at a draft and asking whether each sentence earned its place.

That shift changes what writing feels like. It doesn't change what matters. The ideas still have to be yours. The fear still has to be real. The specific details still have to come from your actual life. "Make it weirder" can't fix a post that has nothing to say. It can only make a post that has something to say sound less like a machine saying it.

One out of 255 noticed. The other 254 argued about the ideas.

I'll take that.
