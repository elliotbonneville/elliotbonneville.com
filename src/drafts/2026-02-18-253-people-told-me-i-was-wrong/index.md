---
title: 253 People Told Me I Was Wrong
date: 2026-02-18
description: My post about money being the only moat hit #1 on Hacker News. 253 comments later, I'm less sure about everything.
---

The traffic counter hit 1,700 concurrent readers before I'd even processed what was happening. My blog is a $6 DigitalOcean droplet serving static HTML. I watched the number climb and waited for the server to catch fire. It didn't. Nginx doesn't care about your feelings.

The post was "[The Only Moat Left Is Money](/the-only-moat-left-is-money/)." I'd written it that morning from a thought I'd had in the bathroom, then rushed out to work and pushed it live. By lunch it was [#1 on Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47062521).

253 comments. Almost all of them telling me I was wrong.

The first objection was definitional. "Money can't be a moat," multiple people wrote, "because you can always get money." Take on debt. Raise a round. Sell equity. A moat, by definition, is something others can't just go acquire. User derf_ put it cleanly: "If someone gave you a billion dollars, could you go compete with that business? If yes, then the business has no moat."

That's true in the Coca-Cola sense. Less true if you're a guy with three kids trying to launch a social network from his living room. But point taken.

The more interesting objections came in a second wave. People proposed alternative moats. Creativity. Taste. Long-built relationships. The kind of deep domain knowledge you can't fake. One person invoked Nassim Taleb: do something that doesn't scale. Be a tailor. Work with your hands.

That comment spawned a 40-reply sub-thread about whether tailoring is already dead. Someone wrote a 500-word elegy for the profession. Someone else said tailors are doing fine if you know where to look. The whole conversation drifted sideways in a way that made me oddly happy, the way internet arguments always do when they care about the wrong thing so passionately it circles back around to being the right thing.

The comment I keep returning to is from user atomicnumber3:

> "The value of human thinking is going down." No! I fundamentally reject this. The value of unoriginal thinking has gone down. Thinking which is quotidian and pedestrian has become even more worthless than it already was. The value of true, original human thinking has gone up even higher than it ever has been.

That's the correction I needed. I'd said thinking was losing value. What I should have said: thinking that a machine can replicate was never as valuable as we thought it was. The quotidian stuff. "Best practices" and "industry standards" and the middle-management synthesis that passes for insight in most meeting rooms. That was commodity work wearing a costume. AI just ripped the costume off.

The other kind of thinking, the kind that happens at 7am while your kids are jumping on their beds and you're staring at the ceiling trying to figure out if the future is going to look anything like what you planned for, that got more valuable. No model was trained on your particular dread. Your bathroom is not in the dataset.

Another commenter, ctoth, wrote what amounted to a manifesto. The gist: everyone in this thread is talking about software like it means a web app with a pricing page and MRR. But the physical world is barely instrumented. Farmers making irrigation decisions on vibes. Crumbling water infrastructure. Buildings bleeding energy because nobody wrote the monitoring software.

"Go talk to a nurse, a farmer, a building inspector," ctoth wrote. "Ask them what's broken. I promise the answer isn't 'nothing' and I promise nobody on ProductHunt is solving it."

That one stung a little. We're all sitting here debating whether SaaS is dead while actual problems wait for someone to look up from their laptop.

A few people called the post what it partly was: a Trojan horse for [Kith](https://joinkith.com), which I'd linked in a postscript. They weren't wrong. One person said a paid, invite-only social network wouldn't have worked 20 years ago either. Fair.

And two people said the post sounded like it was written by AI.

They were right about that too.

I used Claude to write it. The ideas were mine. The fear about money and reach and who gets to build a future, that's real, that's been keeping me up at night for weeks. But the prose was the model's. I edited it, argued with it, kept saying "make it weirder" because the default output is the most statistically average version of every sentence. Even after all that, some readers could still tell. There's something in the cadence that doesn't quite land like a person talking. I haven't figured out how to close that gap yet.

So here's where I am.

I wrote a post saying money is the only moat. It hit #1 on Hacker News. I spent zero dollars getting it there. The post disproved itself by existing.

It got reach because I said something I actually believed. Not because it was polished or optimized or strategically timed. Because it was a real fear, written close to the bone, posted before I could talk myself out of it. That's not a moat you can buy.

The commenters said the moat is creativity, originality, real problems, hard things. I think they're mostly right. But I also notice that most of us, most of the time, don't do those things. We refresh analytics dashboards. Scroll Twitter for two hours and call it market research. Tinker with landing pages until midnight. All the easy versions of the hard thing, and then we wonder why the hard thing never gets done.

Money buys attention. But it can't buy the willingness to sit with a problem until the problem starts to change shape. Maybe that's the moat.

253 people told me I was wrong. I'm still not sure I was. I'm just less sure about what I was right about.
